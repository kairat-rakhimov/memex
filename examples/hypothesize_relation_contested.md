# Hypotheses — green hydrogen production

This sweep assembles a set of system-level hypotheses about how green hydrogen production, infrastructure choices, and policy design might interact with gas markets, power systems, and trade. They are structured conjectures grounded in current literature and modeling, but many of the mechanisms—especially those involving lock-in, investment behavior, and demand formation—remain speculative and contingent on future policy and market responses.

The hypotheses span infrastructure lock-in risks, EU import rules, interactions between hydrogen and natural gas under carbon constraints, the role of flexible electrolysers in power systems, transitional blue hydrogen, ammonia-based export chains, electrolyser technology choice for offshore wind, and the impact of methane leakage on “green” grid hydrogen. Each should be treated as a lead for further analysis rather than as an established result.

## H1 · Low-percentage grid blending as a potential lock-in pathway — score 0.88

*relation_contested · novelty 0.80 · plausibility 0.85 · support 0.84 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** In regions that initially deploy hydrogen mainly via low-percentage blending (≤2% by volume) into natural gas grids as a power-to-gas storage route, this pathway may substantially delay the scale-up of dedicated green hydrogen production and full fuel switching in industry and buildings, unless policies explicitly cap blending as a transitional measure and prioritize pure-hydrogen applications.

**Rationale.** The evidence shows that hydrogen from electrolysis can technically replace natural gas as a thermal energy vector in industry with limited retrofits, that policy and industry narratives often envision converting existing gas grids to carry hydrogen with end-users swapping appliances, that current power-to-gas projects typically inject only very small hydrogen fractions (around 2% by volume) into gas networks due to infrastructure and appliance constraints, and that most hydrogen today is still produced from natural gas with green hydrogen cost targets framed against fossil-derived hydrogen rather than against natural gas itself. These points establish technical feasibility of substitution, current blending practice, and prevailing cost benchmarks, but they do not empirically demonstrate a lock-in effect. The hypothesized mechanism is that, under these conditions, utilities, regulators, and investors could normalize low-percentage blending as a primary decarbonization and storage strategy. Capital and regulatory effort might then flow into monitoring and adapting legacy gas networks for small blends instead of building dedicated green hydrogen production, transport, and end-use systems that enable full fuel switching. Because a few percent blending yields modest emissions reductions while preserving natural gas demand and infrastructure, it could become a politically attractive but suboptimal equilibrium. This lock-in dynamic remains speculative and would depend on future policy choices, regulatory design, and investor behavior rather than being directly evidenced by current projects.

## H2 · Stringent EU import rules and demand for green hydrogen — score 0.85

*relation_contested · novelty 0.70 · plausibility 0.85 · support 0.79 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** Strict enforcement of the EU’s delegated-act definition of green hydrogen for imports, including hourly temporal correlation and additionality, may shift optimal export project design in supplier countries toward more capital-intensive, storage-heavy systems and raise delivered hydrogen costs enough that the EU’s 2030 target of 10 Mt/year renewable hydrogen imports becomes more likely to be binding on the demand side unless accompanied by stronger demand-side quotas and/or subsidies.

**Rationale.** EU policy defines imported green hydrogen via stringent RFNBO criteria—additionality, hourly temporal correlation, and geographic correlation for renewable electricity used in electrolysis—while also setting quantitative targets and quotas for green hydrogen use in EU industry and transport. Modeling work indicates that, without strong policy support, green hydrogen plays a limited role in cost-optimal 2030 decarbonization pathways, and that achieving EU production targets will require financial incentives. For exporters such as Morocco, aligning with the EU definition pushes project designs toward dedicated renewables plus storage and careful temporal matching, which is expected to raise levelized costs relative to laxer definitions that allow more grid reliance or annual matching. Higher delivered costs, in turn, reduce the likelihood that purely market-driven EU demand will naturally reach the 10 Mt/year import target by 2030. Instead, demand at that scale may only materialize if reinforced by binding quotas (e.g., mandated green hydrogen shares in industry and electrofuel mandates in aviation and maritime) and/or financial incentives that bridge the cost gap. The claim that the 10 Mt/year import target becomes binding on the demand side is therefore framed as a possibility rather than a demonstrated outcome: it depends on how responsive EU consumers are to price, how generous future subsidies are, and how quickly technology costs fall, none of which are fully determined by the current evidence.

## H3 · Maximizing green hydrogen and medium-term gas use for heat — score 0.84

*relation_contested · novelty 0.70 · plausibility 0.86 · support 0.73 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** In European decarbonization pathways constrained by CO₂ budgets and carbon storage limits, aggressively maximizing green hydrogen production for synthetic fuels and hard-to-electrify sectors may increase medium-term natural gas use for building and industrial heat relative to more moderate green hydrogen deployment, because it relaxes oil constraints and diverts zero-carbon electricity away from electrifying heat.

**Rationale.** System-level modeling of European energy systems indicates that when green hydrogen production is pushed very high, synthetic fuels derived from green hydrogen can displace fossil oil, which frees up emissions and CO₂ storage headroom that can then be used for additional natural gas combustion, primarily in heating. At the same time, large-scale electrolysis draws heavily on emissions-free electricity, leaving relatively less clean electricity available for direct electrification of heat (e.g., via heat pumps), which slows the shift away from gas heating. The literature also emphasizes that green hydrogen has much lower lifecycle emissions than natural-gas-based hydrogen and is positioned as a competitor and replacement for natural gas in many end uses, which might lead policymakers to assume that maximizing its deployment will monotonically reduce fossil gas demand. The modeling suggests that the opposite can occur under binding CO₂ and storage constraints: prioritizing green hydrogen for synthetic fuels and certain industrial uses can indirectly lock in or even raise natural gas use in the heating sector over the next few decades. This is a contested, model-dependent result, and its relevance to real-world policy will depend on how closely actual systems resemble the modeled assumptions about sector coupling, technology costs, and policy constraints.

## H4 · Price-responsive electrolysers as complementary flexible loads — score 0.83

*relation_contested · novelty 0.60 · plausibility 0.82 · support 0.88 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.86)*

**Hypothesis.** In power systems with growing shares of variable renewables, deploying grid-connected electrolysers for green hydrogen production primarily as flexible, curtailable loads may increase total renewable build-out and reduce curtailment without materially slowing direct electrification, provided that electrolysers operate only when wholesale prices fall below a threshold tied to the long-run levelized cost of renewable generation and that policy support for green hydrogen is conditioned on such price-responsive operation rather than flat production subsidies.

**Rationale.** The evidence establishes that green hydrogen is produced by electrolysis using renewable electricity and that, on an energy basis, it is structurally more expensive than the renewable electricity input itself. This implies that, in competitive markets, green hydrogen cannot generally outbid end-users of electricity for scarce renewable power without eroding its own economics. At the same time, green hydrogen development is described as either a competitor or an opportunity relative to renewable electricity, reflecting that electrolysers can either divert limited clean power away from direct electrification or act as flexible demand that soaks up surplus generation. If electrolysers are required to operate only when prices are low (indicating surplus or otherwise curtailed renewables) and to shut down when prices rise (indicating scarcity and high-value direct uses), they effectively become a sink for excess renewable output rather than a standing competitor for clean electricity. This price-responsive, flexible operation should, in principle, improve the revenue profile of renewable generators, encouraging additional capacity investment, and limit the extent to which hydrogen production cannibalizes electricity available for direct electrification. However, the outcome that this happens “without materially slowing direct electrification” is not directly demonstrated in the cited sources and is contingent on broader market design, the elasticity and timing of other loads, and how electrolysers actually respond to price signals in practice.

## H5 · Suboptimal blue hydrogen as a bridge that may delay green hydrogen — score 0.82

*relation_contested · novelty 0.60 · plausibility 0.84 · support 0.79 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.90)*

**Hypothesis.** Because many existing blue hydrogen projects have not exceeded around 60% CO₂ capture at the SMR plant level, scaling blue hydrogen as a ‘bridge’ fuel in hard-to-abate sectors may materially delay green hydrogen deployment unless policy standards ratchet capture performance toward roughly ≥85% and explicitly constrain upstream methane leakage.

**Rationale.** The sources define blue hydrogen as SMR-based hydrogen with added carbon capture and storage and note that, to date, projects have not achieved capture rates in excess of about 60%, leaving substantial residual CO₂ emissions. When combined with upstream methane leakage, this can give blue hydrogen a significant lifecycle footprint, even though it can leverage existing gas infrastructure and may be cheaper than green hydrogen in the near term. If such suboptimal blue hydrogen is widely adopted to supply emerging hydrogen demand in steel, refining, and power, it risks locking in SMR+CCS assets and gas supply chains for decades, creating economic and political inertia against rapid green hydrogen scale-up. Tightening standards toward higher capture rates (e.g., around 85% or more, as implied by some low-carbon benchmarks) and limiting methane leakage would reduce blue hydrogen’s emissions, making it a more compatible bridge that does not significantly erode the climate advantage of later green hydrogen substitution. The lock-in and delay effects are hypothesized based on general insights about infrastructure and policy inertia; they are not directly observed in current blue hydrogen deployments and would depend on future investment patterns and regulatory choices.

## H6 · Ammonia-based export chains to lower delivered hydrogen costs — score 0.80

*relation_contested · novelty 0.50 · plausibility 0.82 · support 0.85 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.86)*

**Hypothesis.** For export-oriented green hydrogen projects, co-optimizing ammonia synthesis and dehydrogenation as an integrated value chain may reduce the levelized cost of delivered hydrogen by more than about 10% compared with exporting liquefied or gaseous hydrogen alone, particularly in regions with cheap renewable power but limited pipeline access.

**Rationale.** The evidence indicates that a large share of current hydrogen is used as feedstock for ammonia production and that green hydrogen is expected to play a growing role as industrial feedstock for low-carbon ammonia and chemicals. Ammonia synthesis is explicitly considered as a downstream derivative route for exported hydrogen, offering cheaper storage than hydrogen itself and additional system flexibility, and ammonia can be used as a hydrogen carrier, with hydrogen later separated from ammonia using membrane-based cracking technology that has been demonstrated for vehicle fueling. Together, these facts suggest that in regions with abundant low-cost renewable electricity but limited hydrogen pipeline infrastructure, it may be economically advantageous to design green hydrogen export systems around ammonia as both a product and a transport vector: hydrogen is synthesized to ammonia near the production site, stored and shipped as ammonia (benefiting from lower storage and transport costs), and then either used directly as ammonia (e.g., fertilizer, shipping fuel) or cracked back to hydrogen at the point of use. Co-optimizing the sizing and operation of electrolysers, Haber–Bosch units, storage tanks, shipping, and cracking units could therefore lower the levelized cost of delivered hydrogen relative to systems that export hydrogen only as liquefied or compressed gas, by exploiting cheaper storage, higher asset utilization, and multiple revenue streams. The specific magnitude of cost reduction (e.g., >10%) is conjectural and will depend on technology costs, scale, and market conditions.

## H7 · PEM electrolysers versus SOE for offshore wind hydrogen — score 0.75

*relation_contested · novelty 0.40 · plausibility 0.82 · support 0.72 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.86)*

**Hypothesis.** For offshore wind-powered green hydrogen plants operating under highly variable power input, proton exchange membrane electrolysers are likely to yield a lower levelised cost of hydrogen than solid oxide electrolysers, despite SOE’s higher thermodynamic efficiency, because PEM’s higher operating pressure, lower specific electricity consumption for compression, and better dynamic response can increase achievable capacity factor and reduce balance-of-plant and degradation costs enough to outweigh SOE’s efficiency advantage.

**Rationale.** Onshore/offshore techno-economic comparisons report that solid oxide electrolysers exhibit higher stack efficiency but suffer from higher capital expenditure at small scales, lower operating pressure that necessitates external compression, and higher degradation rates, which together can raise their levelised cost of hydrogen relative to PEM in offshore wind hub configurations. Another study on operational optimisation with external heat integration finds that PEM electrolysers, while not necessarily more energy-efficient than SOE at the stack level, can become more economically viable in certain scenarios due to lower capital costs and longer lifetimes, and that PEM efficiency is strongly influenced by power loading, making partial loading a viable strategy near marginal cost. A wind–electrolyser system study explicitly selects PEM over alkaline and solid oxide because its high current density, fast response, and high operating pressure reduce compression and storage needs and make it more suitable for variable renewable energy applications. Together, these findings suggest that under the fluctuating, curtailed power profiles typical of offshore wind, PEM’s dynamic operability and reduced compression energy allow higher effective capacity factors and lower balance-of-plant and replacement costs, which may more than compensate for SOE’s higher conversion efficiency, leading to a lower LCOH for PEM in that specific context. This remains a technology- and context-dependent hypothesis rather than a universal ranking of electrolyser types.

## H8 · Methane leakage and shale gas for grid-powered hydrogen — score 0.71

*relation_contested · novelty 0.70 · plausibility 0.82 · support 0.72 · structure 0.62 · verified (confidence 0.86)*

**Hypothesis.** If shale gas is used as the marginal fuel to power grid-connected electrolysers, then in regions where shale gas has high fugitive methane leakage approaching upper literature estimates, switching coal-based electricity to shale-gas-based electricity for green hydrogen production may not reduce, and could increase, the 20-year life-cycle greenhouse gas footprint per kilogram of hydrogen compared with continuing to use coal-based electricity, whereas in regions with tightly regulated, low-leakage shale gas, the same switch is likely to roughly halve that footprint.

**Rationale.** Most life-cycle assessments find that shale gas electricity has about half the greenhouse gas emissions of coal when methane leakage is controlled, but that under high leakage assumptions shale gas can be comparable to or worse than coal on a 20-year global warming potential basis. EPA analysis indicates shale gas emits more methane than conventional gas but still far less total GHG than coal under typical conditions, while other work argues that with reasonable leakage rates and mitigation technologies, shale gas’s greenhouse footprint is roughly one-half to one-third that of coal. These conflicting results imply a threshold behaviour: the net climate benefit of coal-to-shale-gas switching depends critically on actual fugitive methane rates and the time horizon considered. Because grid electricity is the dominant contributor to the carbon intensity of electrolytic hydrogen, using shale-gas-fired power to run electrolysers will inherit this conditional advantage or disadvantage. In high-leakage shale plays, the effective GHG intensity of electricity can approach or exceed that of coal on a 20-year basis, erasing the expected benefit of displacing coal and potentially making hydrogen produced from such electricity no better, or worse, than hydrogen produced using unabated coal-based power. In contrast, in jurisdictions with stringent regulation and low leakage, the roughly two-fold GHG advantage of shale gas over coal will translate into a similar reduction in the life-cycle footprint of grid-powered hydrogen. This threshold behavior is well grounded in the cited literature, but the actual regime a given region falls into depends on empirical leakage measurements and regulatory effectiveness.
