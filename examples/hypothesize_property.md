# Hypotheses — green hydrogen production

This sweep on green hydrogen production surfaces several policy, infrastructure, and techno‑design hypotheses about where early green hydrogen might create the most impact. They emphasize demand concentration, sector targeting, and system-level integration rather than treating hydrogen as a generic fuel.

All of the points below should be read as tentative leads for further analysis and modeling, not as established results. Each hypothesis highlights a plausible mechanism and outlines ways it could be tested or refuted with additional evidence.

## H1 · Prioritizing hard-to-electrify segments over hydrogen passenger cars — score 0.88

*property · novelty 0.76 · plausibility 0.90 · support 0.81 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** Green hydrogen deployment strategies that prioritize high-utilization, hard-to-electrify segments (such as heavy-duty trucks, buses, and existing industrial hydrogen users) rather than light-duty passenger fuel cell cars may generate significantly higher near-term green hydrogen demand and learning-by-doing benefits, because plug-in electric vehicles already dominate the light-duty segment by roughly three orders of magnitude.

**Rationale.** Vehicle stock data indicate that plug-in electric vehicles number in the tens of millions globally, while fuel cell electric vehicles in the light-duty segment remain in the tens of thousands. This suggests that battery-electric drivetrains have already captured most of the decarbonization trajectory for light-duty road transport, leaving hydrogen cars as a niche with low fleet size and typically modest utilization. Early green hydrogen supply and infrastructure are capital-intensive and limited, so tying scale-up to a small, dispersed application risks weak and geographically fragmented demand signals, which could slow investment and learning. In contrast, heavy-duty transport and incumbent industrial hydrogen users consume large, continuous volumes of energy or hydrogen at relatively few sites, which can support high load factors for electrolysers, pipelines, and storage. Focusing on these segments could therefore create concentrated, bankable offtake that accelerates cost reductions and infrastructure build-out, whereas prioritizing hydrogen passenger cars may underperform on both demand creation and learning-by-doing.

Sensitivity analyses could vary assumptions about future fuel cell car adoption to test whether any plausible passenger-car uptake trajectory reverses the advantage of targeting hard-to-electrify segments first.

## H2 · Binding end-use quotas as a driver of green hydrogen scale — score 0.84

*property · novelty 0.78 · plausibility 0.78 · support 0.74 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.78)*

**Hypothesis.** Because low-emission hydrogen still accounts for less than 1% of both global hydrogen production and hydrogen demand for fuel/feedstock, introducing binding green hydrogen consumption quotas in major industrial hydrogen applications might unlock substantially more green hydrogen deployment than relying on supply-side subsidies alone.

**Rationale.** Despite growing project announcements and evidence of potential cost reductions for green hydrogen toward mid-century, low-emission hydrogen remains below 1% of both supply and demand. This symmetry suggests that the bottleneck is not only high production cost but also weak demand pull in existing hydrogen-using sectors. Current policy mixes emphasize supply-side support and general decarbonization signals, yet they have not produced large-scale switching from fossil-based hydrogen to low-emission alternatives. Binding consumption quotas in sectors such as ammonia, refining, and steel would create guaranteed offtake, improving project bankability and enabling economies of scale and learning that pure subsidies may not achieve if buyers can continue using cheaper fossil hydrogen. This is therefore framed as a policy design hypothesis: that enforceable demand mandates in incumbent hydrogen applications are a missing lever that could help shift the system out of its current low-adoption equilibrium, complementing rather than replacing supply-side support.

## H3 · Variable-speed modular compression in offshore wind–H2 plants — score 0.79

*property · novelty 0.80 · plausibility 0.85 · support 0.70 · structure 0.78 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** In offshore wind–coupled green hydrogen plants, implementing variable-speed, modular compressor trains that maintain near-constant isentropic efficiency across fluctuating hydrogen flows could raise effective compression efficiency well above pessimistic 50% assumptions toward values closer to those seen under steady operation (around 75–80%), with illustrative estimates suggesting a potential reduction in specific electricity consumption for compression on the order of one-third and a corresponding improvement in overall plant electrical-to-hydrogen efficiency by several percentage points.

**Rationale.** Some offshore wind–hydrogen studies assume low compression efficiencies (around 50%) explicitly because frequent load variations from variable wind drive compressors far from their design point, degrading performance. Other analyses under more stable operating conditions report isentropic efficiencies near 80% with high mechanical efficiency, corresponding to relatively low specific compression energy. This contrast suggests that the efficiency gap is largely due to off-design, part-load operation rather than inherent limits of hydrogen compression. Variable-speed drives and modular parallel compressor trains can, in principle, dynamically adjust which compressors are active and at what speed, keeping each unit closer to its optimal operating region even as hydrogen flow fluctuates with wind output. The quantitative uplift from 50% toward 75–80% efficiency and the associated 30–40% reduction in compression electricity use are extrapolated by comparing separate studies and should be treated as approximate, illustrative magnitudes rather than demonstrated outcomes; they highlight the potential scale of benefit if high isentropic efficiency can be maintained under variable loads.

## H4 · Portfolio-scale renewables and shared infrastructure for hydrogen — score 0.75

*property · novelty 0.74 · plausibility 0.85 · support 0.80 · structure 0.62 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** Mega-scale, diversified renewable portfolios that integrate solar, wind, and supporting infrastructure such as desalination may enable green hydrogen projects to operate at higher electrolyser capacity factors and lower levelized hydrogen costs than projects developed by smaller, single-technology players, by internally balancing variable generation and co-optimizing shared infrastructure; this is a theoretical mechanism suggested by early mega-project designs rather than a pattern yet demonstrated across many projects.

**Rationale.** Large developers with tens of gigawatts of solar, wind, and water infrastructure can dedicate multi-gigawatt renewable blocks to electrolysis while using portfolio diversity to smooth power supply. Combining solar and wind, potentially with storage, can reduce variability at the plant level and keep electrolysers running at higher utilization without relying heavily on grid imports. Co-located desalination and shared infrastructure such as ports, transmission, and water intake can be sized and financed across many gigawatts of assets, lowering per-unit costs of both power and water for hydrogen production. Examples like ACWA Power’s portfolio and its participation in large green hydrogen projects illustrate how such integration might work, but they do not yet provide broad empirical evidence that portfolio-scale developers systematically achieve higher capacity factors or lower levelized costs than smaller players. The hypothesis is therefore that portfolio scale and diversity could confer a structural cost advantage for green hydrogen production through higher capacity factors and shared infrastructure economies of scale, which remains to be rigorously tested.

## H5 · Co-locating hydrogen refueling with DC fast-charging corridors — score 0.73

*property · novelty 0.77 · plausibility 0.84 · support 0.71 · structure 0.62 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** In North America, early green hydrogen refueling networks that co-locate with existing high-utilization DC fast-charging corridors and hubs might achieve higher station utilization and lower levelized hydrogen costs than hydrogen-only networks sited mainly around current fuel cell vehicle hotspots such as California.

**Rationale.** Public battery-electric charging infrastructure in North America already consists of tens of thousands of stations, while publicly accessible hydrogen refueling stations number only in the dozens, concentrated largely in a single state. This disparity implies that the siting, permitting, and grid-connection knowledge embedded in the existing fast-charging network is far more extensive than for hydrogen. Co-locating hydrogen refueling with established high-throughput charging sites and freight corridors could leverage existing rights-of-way, strong grid connections, and demonstrated vehicle flows, increasing the likelihood that hydrogen stations see sufficient truck and bus traffic to operate near design capacity. Higher utilization would spread capital and fixed operating costs over more kilograms of hydrogen sold, reducing levelized cost compared with greenfield hydrogen-only sites chosen primarily based on current fuel cell vehicle registrations, which may remain sparse and lead to chronic underutilization. However, this remains an inference from infrastructure patterns and basic economics; direct empirical evidence that co-location systematically yields higher utilization and lower costs is not yet available and would need to come from targeted pilots and comparative studies.

## H6 · Co-optimizing German hydrogen production with HDV highway demand — score 0.72

*property · novelty 0.72 · plausibility 0.78 · support 0.78 · structure 0.62 · verified (confidence 0.86)*

**Hypothesis.** If Germany co-optimizes the siting of green hydrogen production with the heavy-duty vehicle highway refueling network rather than focusing primarily on existing industrial clusters, then by 2050 the total hydrogen transport work and associated energy losses could be reduced disproportionately relative to the long national highway network length, because refueling demand is distributed along the network rather than concentrated at a few nodes.

**Rationale.** The German highway network spans roughly 13,000 km and serves as the backbone for a planned heavy-duty vehicle hydrogen refueling system. While this length is large compared with typical point-to-point industrial transport distances, heavy-duty hydrogen demand is spread along many corridors rather than concentrated at single industrial hubs. If electrolysers are sited near highway nodes with high projected heavy-duty demand and good grid access, hydrogen can be produced closer to where it is dispensed, reducing average transport distance per kilogram even though the overall network is extensive. Additionally, transmitting electricity over long distances to distributed production sites can be more efficient than transporting hydrogen from centralized industrial clusters. The cited German HDV network study establishes the scale and structure of the highway and refueling network and its interaction with the power system, but it does not directly quantify the reduction in total hydrogen transport work from such co-optimization; that reduction is a modeled expectation that follows from basic logistics and power-system considerations and remains to be explicitly demonstrated in comparative scenarios.

## H7 · Climate benefit of hydrogen in CO2-dominated building emissions — score 0.65

*property · novelty 0.69 · plausibility 0.85 · support 0.58 · structure 0.50 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** Because over 95% of building-sector greenhouse gas emissions are carbon dioxide rather than non-CO2 gases, deploying green hydrogen for building energy uses could, in principle, deliver greater climate benefit per unit of fossil energy displaced than in sectors where non-CO2 gases make up a larger share of emissions, provided that hydrogen production has very low upstream methane and nitrous oxide emissions; however, this does not imply that buildings are necessarily a priority sector for hydrogen once cost-effectiveness and strong alternatives like direct electrification are considered.

**Rationale.** The building sector’s greenhouse gas emissions are overwhelmingly CO2, with only a small fraction from methane, nitrous oxide, and halocarbons. Green hydrogen primarily displaces CO2 emissions from combustion of fossil fuels used for heating and high-temperature processes, but it has limited direct impact on non-CO2 process emissions. In sectors where non-CO2 gases constitute a large share of total emissions, switching to green hydrogen addresses only part of the climate problem, leaving substantial residual non-CO2 emissions. In contrast, in buildings where nearly all emissions are CO2 from energy use, each unit of fossil energy replaced by green hydrogen could translate more directly into reductions in total CO2-equivalent emissions, assuming near-zero upstream leakage. At the same time, much of the literature emphasizes that direct electrification (e.g., heat pumps) and efficiency measures often provide cheaper and more energy-efficient decarbonization pathways for buildings than hydrogen. The hypothesis here is therefore narrowly about emissions composition and potential climate benefit per unit of fossil energy displaced, not a blanket claim that buildings are the most favorable or cost-effective sector for hydrogen deployment.

**How to test or refute.** Conduct lifecycle assessment studies comparing greenhouse gas abatement per unit of final energy shifted to green hydrogen across multiple sectors, including buildings, industry, and transport, using consistent assumptions about hydrogen production pathways and upstream emissions. For buildings, model different end-use applications (space heating, water heating, high-temperature processes) and compare hydrogen-based solutions with alternatives such as electrification and efficiency measures, including cost and energy-efficiency metrics. Sensitivity analyses should vary upstream methane leakage and nitrous oxide emissions to identify thresholds at which the relative climate benefit of hydrogen in buildings diminishes. Empirical data from demonstration projects that use green hydrogen in building applications can be used to validate modeled emission reductions against measured fuel use and leakage rates, and to compare performance with electrified alternatives.

Across these hypotheses, a common theme is that green hydrogen’s near-term impact may depend less on its generic technical potential and more on strategic system design: concentrating demand in hard-to-electrify and incumbent hydrogen-using sectors, leveraging existing infrastructure and large renewable portfolios, and co-optimizing spatial siting with power and transport networks. Another cross-cutting insight is the importance of policy and lifecycle framing—binding-style demand mandates and strict control of upstream emissions are posited as potentially important levers, but their actual influence on where green hydrogen delivers the greatest climate and economic value remains to be demonstrated through comparative modeling and empirical evidence.
