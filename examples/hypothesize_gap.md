# Hypotheses — green hydrogen production

This sweep surfaces several tentative mechanisms by which green hydrogen production, storage, and system integration might be improved, spanning materials for hydrogen carriers, infrastructure choices, and operational strategies in highly renewable systems. The hypotheses are grounded in specific modeled or chemical relationships, but in most cases the proposed system-level cycles or performance gains are extrapolated beyond what the cited sources directly demonstrate.

All of these should be treated as exploratory leads rather than established results. Where the underlying literature does not yet quantify a given effect (e.g., full closed carrier cycles, cost impacts, or control-performance gains), this is noted explicitly so that follow-on work can target the main uncertainties.

## H1 · Ionic hydrides as regenerable hydrogen carrier candidates — score 0.74

*gap · novelty 0.60 · plausibility 0.78 · support 0.82 · structure 0.78 · verified (confidence 0.86)*

**Hypothesis.** Ionic hydrides of group 1 and group 2 metals could, in principle, serve as high-density, regenerable hydrogen carriers for green hydrogen systems, where controlled hydrolysis of the hydride in water produces H2 on demand and the resulting metal hydroxide or oxide is returned to a central facility for possible electrochemical reconversion to the hydride using renewable electricity.

**Rationale.** Group 1 and 2 salt-like hydrides contain hydride anions bound to highly electropositive metals, and such hydrides are known to react with water to release hydrogen while forming metal hydroxides or oxides. This suggests a conceptual carrier cycle: hydrides are synthesized from metal compounds using electricity, transported as solid or slurry carriers, and then reacted with water at the point of use to release hydrogen; the spent metal hydroxide or oxide is collected and shipped back for regeneration. The grounded evidence supports the existence of hydride anions in these compounds and the general feasibility of producing hydrogen from water using electricity, but it does not yet demonstrate a practical, closed electrochemical hydride↔hydroxide cycle at scale. Thus, the idea of a regenerable metal–water hydrogen carrier based on these hydrides is an extrapolation from basic chemistry and electrolysis principles, and its viability will hinge on unresolved issues such as regeneration overpotentials, side reactions, material stability, safety, and logistics.

**How to test or refute.** Experimentally, select representative group 1 and 2 hydrides and quantify: (1) hydrogen release rates, yields, and controllability during hydrolysis under practical conditions; (2) energy efficiency, current density, and durability of electrochemical regeneration from the corresponding hydroxides or oxides under realistic, variable renewable power profiles; and (3) material stability, cycling losses, and safety under repeated charge–discharge cycles. Techno-economic modeling should then compare levelized storage and transport costs, round-trip efficiencies, and safety profiles against compressed and liquefied hydrogen and other carriers. System-level simulations can explore logistics for returning spent material and the impact of such a carrier loop on overall emissions and costs, explicitly testing whether a closed, economically viable hydride↔hydroxide cycle is achievable under plausible assumptions.

## H2 · Cheap GH₂ cavern storage reshaping supply chains — score 0.71

*gap · novelty 0.80 · plausibility 0.83 · support 0.56 · structure 0.62 · verified (confidence 0.86)*

**Hypothesis.** If low-cost cavern storage for gaseous hydrogen becomes available in a green hydrogen system, then the cost advantage and temporal flexibility of large-scale GH₂ storage are likely to shift the optimal hydrogen supply chain mix away from liquid hydrogen and liquid organic hydrogen carriers toward gaseous hydrogen, and this shift could in principle increase utilization of surplus renewable electricity while also creating conditions under which low-marginal-cost fossil generation is used more unless CO₂ constraints are sufficiently stringent.

**Rationale.** Modeling work indicates that, under default cost assumptions, capacity deployment of GH₂ storage is limited by high specific investment costs, while liquid hydrogen often dominates as a compromise between efficiency, flexibility, and cost. A sensitivity case with cheap cavern storage shows that low-cost GH₂ cavern storage enables very low-cost, flexible large-scale GH₂ production and substantially increases the share of GH₂ in the modeled supply chains. This supports the part of the hypothesis about a structural shift toward GH₂ when cavern storage is inexpensive. The same study notes that temporally flexible hydrogen supply chains allow electrolysers to shift operation into hours with surplus renewable generation and low electricity prices, which is consistent with increased renewable utilization. The additional clause about potentially higher utilization of low-marginal-cost fossil generation is not directly quantified in the cited work; it is a system-dynamics conjecture based on the general possibility that flexible demand and storage can arbitrage low wholesale prices that may sometimes be set by fossil units. Therefore, the emissions impact of cheap GH₂ cavern storage should be regarded as uncertain and contingent on CO₂ pricing or caps, rather than as a demonstrated rebound effect.

**Grounded in.**
- *cavern storage increases gaseous hydrogen supply chain* — Optimal supply chains and power sector benefits of green hydrogen (http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.03464) · corroborated by 1 source(s) · `relation:986`
  > “Capacity deployment of GH 2 storage is small because of its relatively high specific investment costs. This changes in a sensitivity with cheap cavern storage (see Section SI.2.3).”
- *cavern storage for gaseous hydrogen provides large-scale gaseous hydrogen production* — Optimal supply chains and power sector benefits of green hydrogen (http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.03464) · corroborated by 1 source(s) · `relation:1051`
  > “Low-cost cavern storage would provide flexibility for large-scale GH 2 production at very low costs of 3.5 e/kg, which is about one third of the costs of LOHC or LH 2 storage.”

**How to test or refute.** Use coupled power–hydrogen capacity-expansion and dispatch models to compare scenarios with and without low-cost GH₂ cavern storage, holding other assumptions constant. Track changes in the optimal mix of GH₂, LH₂, and LOHC supply chains, electrolyser deployment, and storage capacities to test the predicted shift toward GH₂. Analyze hourly operation to quantify changes in renewable curtailment, electrolyser full-load hours, and the utilization of fossil generation, explicitly separating effects under weak versus stringent CO₂ constraints. Sensitivity analyses on cavern cost, fossil fuel prices, and carbon policy can identify conditions under which GH₂ cavern storage both increases renewable utilization and, potentially, fossil generation. Where data are available, empirical studies of regions with underground gas or hydrogen storage could be used to look for correlations between storage build-out, curtailment, and fossil plant utilization, while carefully controlling for confounding factors.

## H3 · EV demand-side management sharpening price signals for electrolysis — score 0.67

*gap · novelty 0.75 · plausibility 0.84 · support 0.60 · structure 0.50 · verified (confidence 0.90)*

**Hypothesis.** Allowing demand-side management for passenger electric vehicles in a highly renewable, sector-coupled power system will tend to increase the temporal concentration of low-price surplus electricity into midday hours, thereby raising electrolyser full-load hours at low electricity prices and reducing levelized green hydrogen production costs compared with a case without demand-side management where only electrolysers are flexible.

**Rationale.** In the REMIND–PyPSA–Eur framework, electrolysers already respond strongly to hourly prices in both demand-side management (DSM) and NoDSM cases, operating heavily during midday solar peaks and using hydrogen storage to shift energy seasonally. When DSM is enabled, passenger EV charging is shifted toward low-price hours, which deepens demand troughs at other times and amplifies price spreads between solar-peak hours and the rest of the day. This modeled behavior supports the claim that DSM sharpens diurnal price signals and increases the frequency of very low-price hours. The specific impact on electrolyser full-load hours and levelized cost of hydrogen is not directly reported in the cited work; it is inferred from the modeled price dynamics and the assumed price-responsiveness of electrolysers. Thus, the expectation that DSM will systematically improve electrolyser economics should be treated as a hypothesis derived from model structure rather than as an empirically or numerically demonstrated result, and its magnitude will depend on EV penetration, charging flexibility, and system design.

**Grounded in.**
- *demand-side management scenario enables passenger electric vehicle* — REMIND-PyPSA-Eur: Integrating power system flexibility into sector-coupled energy transition pathways (http://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04388) · corroborated by 1 source(s) · `relation:6118`
  > “In the DSM scenario, sectors shift their demand to hours with lower electricity prices, particularly for passenger EVs.”
- *no demand-side management scenario affects passenger electric vehicle* — REMIND-PyPSA-Eur: Integrating power system flexibility into sector-coupled energy transition pathways (http://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04388) · corroborated by 1 source(s) · `relation:6119`
  > “In the NoDSM scenario, sectors other than electrolysis cannot shift their demand in time.”

**How to test or refute.** In a sector-coupled power system model that includes explicit representations of EV fleets, charging flexibility, and hydrogen electrolysis, run paired scenarios with and without EV demand-side management while keeping renewable capacity and other key assumptions constant. Compare hourly price distributions, electrolyser full-load hours, curtailment, and levelized hydrogen production costs to quantify the effect of DSM on hydrogen economics. Conduct sensitivity analyses over EV adoption levels, charging-window constraints, and hydrogen storage capacities. Where feasible, analyze data from regions with smart EV charging programs and co-located electrolysers to see whether increased price volatility and more frequent low-price hours are associated with higher electrolyser utilization at low electricity prices, recognizing that such empirical evidence may be noisy and confounded.

## H4 · Dynamic real-time optimization for electrolyzer fleets — score 0.61

*gap · novelty 0.70 · plausibility 0.78 · support 0.46 · structure 0.50 · verified (confidence 0.78)*

**Hypothesis.** For systems of grid-connected electrolyzers used for green hydrogen production, extending a static site-wide optimization model into a fully dynamic real-time optimization layer that explicitly updates both renewable availability and electrolyzer state trajectories could yield higher renewable utilization and reduced grid imbalance costs than a simpler real-time optimization that only re-solves the static model with updated inputs.

**Rationale.** Existing work demonstrates that static optimization models for electrolyzer systems can be embedded in a two-stage framework with site-wide optimization and real-time optimization to manage flexible resources, and that transforming static models into dynamic RTO formulations is technically feasible. However, the cited research primarily establishes this methodological potential; it does not yet provide systematic quantitative evidence that a fully dynamic RTO outperforms a quasi-static RTO in terms of renewable utilization or grid imbalance costs. The hypothesized advantage arises from control theory and model predictive control intuition: a dynamic RTO that tracks state trajectories (e.g., ramping limits, start–stop behavior, degradation states) over a moving horizon can pre-emptively adjust electrolyser operation in anticipation of forecast changes, better respect dynamic constraints, and co-optimize short-term operation with medium-term wear and efficiency. These mechanisms suggest that dynamic RTO could improve alignment between electrolyser load and variable renewable generation and reduce reliance on balancing energy, but the size and robustness of these gains remain to be quantified in realistic case studies.

**Grounded in.**
- *static optimization model transformed into real-time optimization model* — Combination of Site-Wide and Real-Time Optimization for the Control of Systems of Electrolyzers (http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06748) · corroborated by 1 source(s) · `relation:5727`
  > “The presented research demonstrates the potential of transforming static optimization models into dynamic, RTO models for managing flexible energy resources such as electrolyzers.”

**How to test or refute.** Implement two control schemes for the same modeled or pilot electrolyzer system: (1) a quasi-static real-time optimization that repeatedly solves a static site-wide model with updated forecasts, and (2) a dynamic real-time optimization (e.g., model predictive control) that includes explicit state trajectories and dynamic constraints over a receding horizon. Using high-resolution renewable generation and price data, simulate both controllers over extended periods and compare metrics such as renewable energy utilization, grid imbalance or reserve costs, electrolyser start–stop frequency, and degradation proxies, as well as computational burden. Where possible, conduct hardware-in-the-loop experiments or field trials on a smaller electrolyser installation to validate simulation results and to assess practical implementation challenges, such as model mismatch, communication delays, and robustness to forecast errors.

Across these hypotheses, a recurring theme is that both material innovations and system-level flexibility could substantially reshape green hydrogen pathways, but the strongest claims currently rest on extrapolations from basic chemistry or from modeled price and control dynamics rather than on fully demonstrated cycles or quantified performance gains. Targeted experiments on metal–water carrier cycles, detailed power–hydrogen system modeling with storage and DSM, and comparative studies of control architectures for electrolyser fleets would help determine which of these speculative levers offer robust, scalable benefits for low-carbon hydrogen deployment.
