# Hypotheses — green hydrogen production

This revised sweep on green hydrogen production summarizes six contested, model-based hypotheses spanning policy design, system planning, infrastructure layout, and operational dynamics. They are structured conjectures grounded in specific studies and data points, not established general results, and several extrapolate from limited regional or technological cases.

Across the hypotheses, a recurring theme is that outcomes for green hydrogen depend strongly on policy constraints, spatial and resource heterogeneity, and modeling boundaries. The language below has been tightened to avoid over-claiming where evidence is indirect, and the speculative nature of several mechanisms is made explicit.

## H1 · Policy mandates may overshoot cost-optimal green H2 shares — score 0.87

*attribute_contested · novelty 0.80 · plausibility 0.85 · support 0.76 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** Sector-specific minimum green hydrogen mandates, such as the EU’s 42% industrial green hydrogen requirement by 2030, will drive the realized share of green hydrogen in those sectors above the levels that would emerge from unconstrained cost-optimal energy-system models, increasing near-term system costs but potentially accelerating learning and infrastructure build-out so that long-run green hydrogen penetration can exceed that in unconstrained baselines.

**Rationale.** Current global green hydrogen deployment remains very small relative to total hydrogen use and even to low-emission hydrogen, reflecting its higher costs and integration challenges. Cost-optimal energy-system modeling without binding green hydrogen quotas often yields relatively modest green shares, even by mid-century, because cheaper low-emission or fossil-based options remain available. In contrast, EU rules impose binding minimum green shares in specific sectors (for example, a 42% green share in industry by 2030), and plant-level optimization under these rules can achieve green fractions well above unconstrained model averages. This supports the idea that mandates can force deployment beyond what short-run cost minimization would choose, raising near-term system costs. Endogenous learning frameworks show that higher early deployment can reduce future costs via learning-by-doing and scale effects, which suggests—but does not yet empirically prove—that such policy-driven overdeployment may shift future cost-optimal mixes toward higher green hydrogen shares than in scenarios without early mandates. The strength and persistence of these learning and infrastructure effects, and whether they actually lead to higher long-run penetration than unconstrained baselines, remain key contested points.

## H2 · Electrolyser fleets can scale faster than proportionally — score 0.85

*attribute_contested · novelty 0.70 · plausibility 0.85 · support 0.79 · structure 1.00 · verified (confidence 0.93)*

**Hypothesis.** In power-system planning models that co-optimise electricity and green hydrogen, the cost-optimal installed electrolyser capacity tends to increase very rapidly—potentially much faster than proportionally—with both decarbonisation stringency and the availability of low-marginal-cost variable renewables, so that high-renewable, high-ambition regions can cost-optimally deploy electrolyser fleets an order of magnitude larger than capacities inferred from project-level plant optimisation.

**Rationale.** Plant-level studies typically optimise a single co-located electrolyser against the output of a specific renewable plant and local market conditions, yielding optimal capacities on the order of 100–1000 MW that are constrained by that plant’s output and local price signals. System-level models, by contrast, treat electrolysers as flexible loads that can absorb surplus wind and solar, reduce curtailment, and displace fossil generation across an entire grid. In such models, as renewable penetration and decarbonisation constraints tighten, the marginal system value of additional flexible demand rises, and optimal electrolyser capacity can reach several GW in a single country and tens to hundreds of GW across a region. The cited examples of ~5.5 GW in Kenya by 2050 and ~140 GW across Europe by 2040, compared with 100–1000 MW plant-scale cases, are consistent with order-of-magnitude differences. However, they do not rigorously establish a specific superlinear functional form; the conjecture is that, across scenarios, capacity tends to scale much faster than proportionally with climate ambition and renewable availability because of the growing system value of flexibility.

## H3 · Limiting hydropower-rich regions to cheapest H2 potential lowers LCOH — score 0.78

*attribute_contested · novelty 0.75 · plausibility 0.82 · support 0.76 · structure 0.78 · verified (confidence 0.79)*

**Hypothesis.** In regions with abundant low-cost hydropower, limiting green hydrogen production to only the cheapest few percent of technical potential may yield 2050 levelized hydrogen costs roughly 1.5–2 times lower than pursuing near-full exploitation of local renewable potential, because expanding output forces the system to incorporate progressively higher-cost solar and wind configurations that raise average electricity and infrastructure costs per kilogram of hydrogen.

**Rationale.** In the studied Benin case, when hydrogen production is supplied purely by reservoir hydropower, the modeled LCOH is about 1.3 €/kgH2 and remains near that level up to roughly a 2–4% expansion step of the region’s maximum technical hydrogen potential. As production expands beyond this low-hanging potential, the optimized system gradually adds open-field PV and then onshore wind, increasing LCOH first to around 2.1 €/kgH2 and then to about 2.4 €/kgH2 at full exploitation. This implies that the marginal electricity used for additional hydrogen comes from progressively more expensive or less favorably utilized renewables, raising the average cost. The 1.5–2× cost range is therefore extrapolated from this specific case (1.3 vs 2.4 €/kgH2) rather than established across many regions. Additional work for Germany and Kenya, with higher LCOH ranges and strong sensitivity to technology mix and grid integration, supports the broader idea that resource quality and system configuration dominate LCOH. The generality of the specific cost multipliers beyond the Benin case remains uncertain and is a key point to test.

## H4 · Multiple medium H2 stations could beat single XXL under caps — score 0.73

*attribute_contested · novelty 0.80 · plausibility 0.77 · support 0.77 · structure 0.62 · verified (confidence 0.86)*

**Hypothesis.** For heavy-duty vehicle hydrogen refueling networks subject to regulatory caps on low-pressure storage and discrete station size modules, network-level cost and grid impact at high traffic demand may often be lower when deploying multiple medium-sized stations rather than a single very large station, because the low-pressure storage cap limits the effective utilisation of additional high-pressure capacity in XXL configurations.

**Rationale.** The documented modular station designs show discrete jumps in both low- and high-pressure storage, with low-pressure capacity ranging from under 1 tonne to 30 tonnes and a regulatory ceiling of 30 tonnes at 200 bar. As station size increases from XS to XXL, high-pressure buffer capacity grows from about 113 kg to 3,600 kg, but low-pressure storage cannot exceed the 30-tonne cap. At very high demand, an XXL station may therefore hit the low-pressure limit, so further increases in high-pressure capacity and compression power yield diminishing returns, potentially increasing cycling, peak loads, and grid connection requirements. Splitting demand across two or more M or L stations can keep each below the cap, provide similar aggregate high-pressure capacity, and distribute compression and grid loads more evenly, which could reduce total system costs and improve integration with variable green electricity. However, the available evidence documents capacities and regulatory limits, not direct cost or grid-impact comparisons, so the claim that multiple medium stations outperform a single XXL station is a speculative network-design hypothesis that depends on spatial demand patterns, refueling behavior, and detailed cost structures.

## H5 · German road-transport H2 likely a minor driver of EU capacity — score 0.71

*attribute_contested · novelty 0.65 · plausibility 0.82 · support 0.80 · structure 0.62 · verified (confidence 0.86)*

**Hypothesis.** If 5–25% of German private and public road-based passenger traffic energy demand in 2030 is met with green hydrogen, then the resulting additional electricity demand for electrolysis will be modest relative to European-scale green hydrogen demand trajectories (on the order of 10–50 TWh/a versus much larger EU-wide totals), implying that transport-sector hydrogen in Germany is likely to be a relatively small share of, and thus unlikely to be the main driver of, green hydrogen production capacity in most EU-wide planning scenarios.

**Rationale.** German mobility scenarios translate 5%, 10%, and 25% hydrogen penetration in 2030 road passenger traffic into annual hydrogen demands of roughly 9, 18, and 45 TWh at filling stations, corresponding to several hundred kilotonnes of hydrogen. These values represent only the passenger road-transport segment in one country. Sector-coupled European modeling work (not detailed here) typically projects total hydrogen demand rising to the order of hundreds to thousands of TWh per year by 2050 across all sectors. Even if all German road-transport hydrogen were produced via electrolysis, the incremental electricity demand (given typical electrolysis efficiencies) would be a relatively small fraction of the electricity required for overall European green hydrogen production in such scenarios. This supports the qualitative inference that, at the European planning scale, industrial feedstock substitution and power-sector applications are more likely to dominate capacity requirements, while German road-transport hydrogen demand is a secondary driver. This inference could be overturned if transport hydrogen adoption accelerates much faster than current scenarios or if industrial and power-sector hydrogen demand underperforms.

## H6 · Renewable variability, not season, sets H2 ramp-rate envelope — score 0.67

*attribute_contested · novelty 0.60 · plausibility 0.84 · support 0.79 · structure 0.50 · verified (confidence 0.90)*

**Hypothesis.** In off-grid renewable power-to-hydrogen systems with battery-based grid-forming control and fast load-following strategies, the maximum minute-scale rate of change of hydrogen production in each month is governed mainly by short-term renewable variability and control settings rather than by the month’s average hydrogen yield, so low-yield months can still exhibit ramp rates comparable to or higher than high-yield months.

**Rationale.** In the Inner Mongolian case study, monthly maximum hydrogen production ramp rates span a relatively narrow range of roughly 5.5–9.9 kg/min across the year, even though seasonal average hydrogen output is much lower in winter than in summer due to weaker irradiation and lower wind speeds. This indicates that the system’s short-term dynamics are decoupled from seasonal energy availability. The grid-forming battery and the proposed secondary load-following strategy cause electrolyser power to track rapid fluctuations in renewable generation at a 4 s time step, so transient events such as wind gusts or PV intermittency can drive large minute-scale changes in hydrogen output regardless of the month’s total energy. Conversely, months with high average yield but smoother resource profiles or more conservative energy management may not exhibit proportionally higher ramp rates. The generality of this behavior across technologies, control schemes, and climates remains uncertain and is an important target for further study.
